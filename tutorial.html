fretboard tutorial — the tour

Six views, one neck. Everything here is also an interactive toy.

1. Strings and notes

Standard tuning, low to high: E A D G B e. Every fret is one half step. Twelve frets up, the notes repeat an octave higher — the double dot marks the reset. Notice the same note lives in several places: that redundancy is the whole character of the instrument.

The redundancy is easiest to see on a chart from the 19th-century method books (this one follows Aguado's Nuevo Método para Guitarra): every pitch from E2 to E5 is a column, with its position on the staff and, below, the fret where it lives on each string. Reading down a column finds every unison; reading along a string row walks up the neck. Here with B picked out — one color per octave, a convention used everywhere in the toy: same color means the same pitch, different colors are octaves:

Try it: Notes mode — click any position (or any chart column) to light that note up everywhere.

2. The circle of 5ths (and 4ths)

Step clockwise and each key sits a perfect 5th higher, gaining one sharp; step counter-clockwise and you move in 4ths, gaining one flat. Neighbors on the circle share six of their seven notes. The inner ring shows each key's relative minor — same notes, different home.

3. Intervals

An interval has two names. The diatonic name counts letter names (a 3rd spans three letters: G→B) with a quality — major, minor, augmented, diminished. The chromatic name just counts half steps, which on a guitar means frets. Both matter: chords are built from the first, fretboard shapes from the second.

4. Build a chord

A chord is intervals stacked from a root. Here is the open C major shape: the notes are C–E–G, spelled root, major 3rd, perfect 5th.

A chord's shape is easiest to see on the same chromatic ruler from section 3. Major chord tones land on R, 3 and 5; a minor chord's third falls one slot left, on the unshaded ♭3:

The same set of notes can spell more than one chord — add an A under C–E–G and it reads as both Am7 and C6. The builder shows every reading it knows.

Try it: Chord builder — click frets on the neck and it names what you made, with the spelling, the spacing between neighbors, and each tone's interval from the root. The staff and the ruler are clickable too: tap a ruler slot to add that tone (it offers the octave options), tap a staff line to add that pitch.

5. Chords by name — fingerings and arpeggios

The reverse direction: name a chord and the neck answers. Every chord tone lights up across the fretboard — that map is the arpeggio, the chord played one note at a time. A search over the neck then turns up playable fingerings: mutes only at the edges, fretted notes within a four-fret reach, every chord tone present. 6. CAGED — five shapes tile the neck

The five open major shapes — C, A, G, E, D — are the only major chord shapes there are. Slide any of them up the neck (barring what the nut used to do) and it stays the same chord type with a new root. Better: for any one chord, the five shapes connect end to end and tile the whole fretboard. Here is C major through all five positions, low to high — notice each shape begins where the previous one ends, and the octave-colored root notes are the seams:

Lay these over the arpeggio map from section 5 and they account for every marker on the neck. That is the whole CAGED system: five shapes, one octave of neck, repeat.

Minor works the same way — flatten the 3rd in each shape. The Em, Am and Dm grips stay easy; the Cm and Gm ones are famously awkward, which is why most teaching leans on the other three.
